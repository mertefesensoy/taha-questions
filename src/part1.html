PROBLEM 1

Knights on the \(3\times 3\) board

a cyclic-order invariant
Problem

Place two white and two dark knights in the corners of a \(3\times 3\) chessboard so that the identically coloured knights are on diametrically opposite corners. The knights move in the usual way known from chess, and two knights cannot stand on the same square at the same time. Is it possible to move the knights so that they stand in the corners at the end, but the diametrically opposite ones are of different colours?

The answer is no. We prove it by exhibiting a quantity — the cyclic order in which the four knights appear around a certain \(8\)-cycle — that no legal move can ever change, and which differs between the starting position and every admissible target position.

Step 1. Set‑up and the knight‑move graph

Number the squares of the board

1 2 3
4 5 6
7 8 9

so that square \(s\) sits in row \(\lfloor (s-1)/3\rfloor\) and column \((s-1)\bmod 3\). The corners are \(1,3,7,9\); the two "diametrically opposite" corner pairs (opposite through the centre square \(5\)) are \(\{1,9\}\) and \(\{3,7\}\).

Up to swapping the names of the two colours, the starting position is therefore

White on \(1\) and \(9\),   Dark on \(3\) and \(7\).

The required final position is: all four knights on corners, with \(\text{colour}(1)\neq\text{colour}(9)\) and \(\text{colour}(3)\neq\text{colour}(7)\).

Define the knight‑move graph \(K\): its vertices are the nine squares, and two squares are adjacent when they are one knight's move apart, i.e. when their (row, column) vectors differ by \((\pm1,\pm2)\) or \((\pm2,\pm1)\). A knight standing on a square may move exactly along an edge of \(K\), provided the target square is unoccupied. The complete list of moves is short enough to write down in full.

Step 2. The usable squares form a single \(8\)‑cycle

Reading off the table of moves:

{{TBL:KNIGHT}}

Two things are visible at once.

  • The centre square \(5\) is isolated in \(K\): from \((1,1)\) every knight's move \((\pm1,\pm2)\) or \((\pm2,\pm1)\) leaves the \(3\times3\) board. Hence no knight can ever move to \(5\) or away from \(5\). Since the knights start on corners, square \(5\) is empty at the start and stays empty forever; we may delete it.
  • Every one of the remaining eight squares has degree exactly \(2\). So \(K-5\) is a \(2\)-regular graph on \(8\) vertices, and it is connected: starting at \(1\) and repeatedly walking to the not‑yet‑used neighbour gives \[ 1 \;-\; 6 \;-\; 7 \;-\; 2 \;-\; 9 \;-\; 4 \;-\; 3 \;-\; 8 \;-\; 1 , \] a closed walk through all eight squares. Therefore \(K-5\cong C_8\).

(This is the classical "straightening" of the puzzle: the tangle of knight moves on the board is really just a circle. It is also an instance of Problem 3 below: a connected \(2\)-regular graph must be a cycle.)

The crucial structural fact, plainly visible in the figure, is that the corners \(1,7,9,3\) occupy the four alternate vertices \(w_0,w_2,w_4,w_6\) of the cycle \(w_0w_1\dots w_7 = 1\,6\,7\,2\,9\,4\,3\,8\). In particular, when all four knights stand on corners, reading the cycle in its natural direction reads the corners in the cyclic order \(1,\,7,\,9,\,3\).

Step 3. The invariant: four beads on a closed string

Claim Assign to each position the cyclic word obtained by listing the colours of the occupied vertices in the cyclic order of \(C_8\) (a cyclic word of length \(4\) in the letters \(\mathrm{W}, \mathrm{D}\), with two of each). This colour pattern is unchanged by every legal move.
Proof. Fix a position. Let the four knights, listed in the cyclic order in which they occur along \(C_8\), be \(k_1,k_2,k_3,k_4\), and for \(j=1,2,3,4\) let \(a_j\ge 1\) be the number of cycle‑edges on the arc that leads from \(k_j\) forward to \(k_{j+1}\) (indices mod \(4\)). The four arcs tile the cycle, so \[ a_1+a_2+a_3+a_4 \;=\; 8, \qquad a_j\ \ge\ 1 \ \text{ for all } j. \] A legal move takes one knight, say \(k_j\), to one of its two neighbours on \(C_8\), and that neighbour must be unoccupied.
  • If \(k_j\) steps forward (towards \(k_{j+1}\)), the square it steps onto is unoccupied exactly when the arc in front of it has length \(a_j\ge 2\). After the move \(a_j\) becomes \(a_j-1\ \ge 1\) and \(a_{j-1}\) becomes \(a_{j-1}+1\).
  • If \(k_j\) steps backward, symmetrically \(a_{j-1}\ge 2\) is required, and afterwards \(a_{j-1}\mapsto a_{j-1}-1\ge 1\), \(a_j\mapsto a_j+1\).
In both cases all four arc lengths remain \(\ge 1\). An arc length of \(0\) is precisely what it would mean for one knight to catch up with and overtake the next one; since this never happens, the cyclic sequence \(k_1,k_2,k_3,k_4\) — and hence the cyclic sequence of their colours — is literally the same list after the move as before. □

Informally: the knights are four beads threaded on a closed loop of string. A bead may slide one step at a time, but it can never jump over another bead, so the cyclic order of the beads is frozen for all time.

Step 4. The two patterns disagree

By Step 2, whenever all four knights stand on corners the colour pattern is read off the corners in the cyclic order \(1,\,7,\,9,\,3\).

Starting position. \(\text{colour}(1)=\mathrm{W}\), \(\text{colour}(7)=\mathrm{D}\), \(\text{colour}(9)=\mathrm{W}\), \(\text{colour}(3)=\mathrm{D}\), giving the cyclic word

\(\mathrm{W\,D\,W\,D}\) — the two whites are separated by a dark knight on both sides.

Any admissible target position. We need \(\text{colour}(1)\ne\text{colour}(9)\) and \(\text{colour}(3)\ne\text{colour}(7)\). As there are two knights of each colour this leaves exactly four possibilities, and all four give the same cyclic word:

{{TBL:P1TARGET}}

In every case the target pattern is \(\mathrm{W\,W\,D\,D}\) — the two whites are cyclically adjacent.

The cyclic words \(\mathrm{WDWD}\) and \(\mathrm{WWDD}\) are different: in the first, each white knight has a dark knight on either side; in the second, the two whites are neighbours in the cyclic order. No rotation of \(\mathrm{WDWD}\) produces \(\mathrm{WWDD}\). By the Claim of Step 3 the pattern never changes, so no sequence of legal moves can lead from the starting position to any admissible target position.

Answer

No, it is not possible. The eight squares a knight can ever use form a single \(8\)-cycle, on which the four knights behave like beads on a closed string: they can slide but never pass one another, so their cyclic order — and hence the cyclic pattern of their colours — is invariant. Starting from "identically coloured knights diametrically opposite" the pattern is \(\mathrm{WDWD}\), whereas every corner position with "diametrically opposite knights of different colours" has pattern \(\mathrm{WWDD}\).

Step 5. Exhaustive verification

A breadth‑first search over all positions confirms the argument and shows that the invariant is complete, i.e. it is the only obstruction.

{{TBL:P1BFS}}

The two reachable corner positions are the initial one and the one obtained from it by sliding all four knights two steps around the cycle (a \(90^\circ\) rotation of the board); in both, identically coloured knights are diametrically opposite, exactly as predicted. Moreover the number of reachable positions, \(140\), equals precisely the number of placements whose colour pattern is \(\mathrm{WDWD}\) — every position with the correct pattern is reachable, and no other.

Remark (what is possible) Only the cyclic order is frozen; rotations are free. This is why the classical Guarini puzzle (1512) — white knights on the two top corners \(1,3\), dark knights on the two bottom corners \(7,9\), the task being to interchange them — is solvable: reading the corners in the cyclic order \(1,7,9,3\), the start is \(\mathrm{W\,D\,D\,W}\) and the target is \(\mathrm{D\,W\,W\,D}\), and as cyclic words both equal \(\mathrm{WWDD}\). A shortest solution takes \(16\) moves. Guarini's puzzle and the present problem differ only in the initial arrangement, and that single difference decides solvability.

What is assumed

Graphs are finite; they are simple unless the problem explicitly allows otherwise. We write \(n=|V(G)|\), \(m=|E(G)|\), \(d(v)\) for the degree of \(v\), \(\Delta(G)\) and \(\delta(G)\) for the largest and smallest degree, and \(c(G)\) for the number of connected components. \(K_n\), \(P_n\) and \(C_n\) are the complete graph, the path and the cycle on \(n\) vertices, and \(\overline{G}\) is the complement of \(G\). Four facts are used throughout.

Lemma A (handshake lemma) \(\sum_{v\in V(G)} d(v) = 2m\).
Proof. Count the pairs \((v,e)\) with \(v\) an endvertex of \(e\) in two ways: by edges, each contributing \(2\); by vertices, vertex \(v\) contributing \(d(v)\). □
Lemma B (components are degree‑closed) If \(H\) is a component of \(G\) and \(x\in V(H)\), then every neighbour of \(x\) lies in \(H\); hence \(d_H(x)=d_G(x)\) and \(|V(H)|\ge d_G(x)+1\).
Proof. A neighbour \(y\) of \(x\) is joined to \(x\) by an edge, so it lies in the same component. The \(d_G(x)\) neighbours together with \(x\) itself are \(d_G(x)+1\) distinct vertices of \(H\). □
Lemma C (edge addition, EAL) Adding one edge \(e\) to \(G\) either creates no cycle through \(e\), in which case \(c(G+e)=c(G)-1\), or creates a cycle through \(e\), in which case \(c(G+e)=c(G)\). In particular one edge lowers the number of components by at most one.
Lemma D (forests) A forest on \(n\) vertices with \(c\) components has exactly \(n-c\) edges. Consequently a forest has at most \(n-1\) edges, with equality exactly for trees.
Proof. Build the forest edge by edge from the edgeless graph, which has \(n\) components. No added edge ever lies on a cycle, so by Lemma C each addition lowers the component count by exactly one. After \(m\) additions the count is \(n-m\), and this equals \(c\). □
PROBLEM 9

Graphs with all degrees distinct

pigeonhole
Problem

Determine all finite simple graphs that do not have two vertices of the same degree.

Theorem 9.1 The only finite simple graph whose degrees are pairwise distinct is the one‑vertex graph \(K_1\).
Proof. For \(n=1\) the condition is vacuous, and \(K_1\) qualifies. So let \(G\) be simple with \(n\ge2\) vertices and suppose its \(n\) degrees are pairwise distinct.

In a simple graph a vertex has at most \(n-1\) neighbours, so every degree lies in the \(n\)-element set \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\). We have \(n\) pairwise distinct degrees drawn from a set of size \(n\), so every value in \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\) is taken, each exactly once. In particular there is a vertex \(u\) with \(d(u)=0\) and a vertex \(w\) with \(d(w)=n-1\), and \(u\ne w\) because \(0\ne n-1\) (here \(n\ge2\)).

Now \(d(w)=n-1\) means \(w\) is adjacent to all \(n-1\) other vertices, \(u\) among them. But then \(d(u)\ge 1\), contradicting \(d(u)=0\).

So no simple graph on \(n\ge2\) vertices has pairwise distinct degrees.

□
Answer

Only \(K_1\). For \(n\ge2\) the \(n\) distinct degrees would have to exhaust \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\), producing simultaneously a vertex of degree \(0\) and a vertex of degree \(n-1\) — which is impossible, since the latter is adjacent to the former.

The usual formulation Turned around, the theorem says: every simple graph on at least two vertices has two vertices of equal degree. This is the standard "two people at a party have the same number of acquaintances" puzzle. Note where simplicity is used: with loops or parallel edges the degrees are no longer confined to \(\{0,\dots,n-1\}\) and the argument collapses — two vertices joined by one edge and by a second parallel edge attached to a loop can easily have different degrees.
Machine check Every simple graph on \(n\le 6\) vertices was enumerated: exactly one has pairwise distinct degrees, namely \(K_1\) at \(n=1\); for \(n=2,\dots,6\) there are none.
PROBLEM 10

The degree sequence 2, 2, 2, 4, 5, 5

forced adjacencies
Problem

Prove that if the degree sequence of a 6-vertex graph is \(2,2,2,4,5,5\), then it is not simple.

The two cheap tests are passed, so they cannot be the obstruction: the entries sum to \(2+2+2+4+5+5=20\), which is even (so \(m=10\)), and the largest entry \(5\) does not exceed \(n-1=5\). The obstruction is structural, and the two vertices of degree \(5\) are what force it.

Theorem 10.1 No simple graph on six vertices has degree sequence \(2,2,2,4,5,5\).
Proof. Suppose \(G\) were such a simple graph, on the vertex set \(\{a,b,c,d,x,y\}\) with \[ d(a)=d(b)=d(c)=2, \qquad d(d)=4, \qquad d(x)=d(y)=5 . \]

Step 1: \(x\) and \(y\) are joined to everything. Since \(G\) is simple on six vertices, a vertex has at most \(5\) neighbours, and they are distinct. So \(d(x)=5\) forces \(x\) to be adjacent to each of \(a,b,c,d,y\), and likewise \(y\) to each of \(a,b,c,d,x\).

Step 2: \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are used up. By Step 1 each of \(a,b,c\) is already adjacent to both \(x\) and \(y\), so each already has degree \(2\). As their prescribed degree is exactly \(2\), none of them is adjacent to anything else. In particular \[ ad,\; bd,\; cd \notin E(G). \]

Step 3: \(d\) cannot reach degree 4. By Step 1, \(d\) is adjacent to \(x\) and to \(y\); by Step 2 it is adjacent to none of \(a,b,c\). Since \(G\) is simple, \(d\) cannot be joined to \(x\) or \(y\) a second time, and there is no loop at \(d\). Hence \(d(d)=2\), contradicting \(d(d)=4\).

The assumption of simplicity is what fails, so no simple graph has this degree sequence.

□

The same conclusion from Havel–Hakimi

The reduction of Havel and Hakimi deletes the largest entry \(d_1\) and subtracts \(1\) from each of the next \(d_1\) entries; the original sequence is graphical if and only if the reduced one is. Applied here:

At the third step the leading entry \(2\) demands two further vertices of positive residual degree, and none remain. So the sequence is not graphical — in agreement with Theorem 10.1.

Answer

No simple graph has this degree sequence, so any graph realising \(2,2,2,4,5,5\) must contain a loop or a pair of parallel edges. The reason is that the two vertices of degree \(5=n-1\) are adjacent to everything; that already exhausts the degree of the three vertices of degree \(2\), leaving the vertex of prescribed degree \(4\) with only \(x\) and \(y\) available — degree \(2\).

The sequence is realisable if parallel edges are allowed Simplicity is exactly what fails, and nothing more. Taking the edges \[ xy,\; xd,\; xd,\; xa,\; xb,\; yd,\; yd,\; yc,\; ya,\; bc \] (with \(xd\) and \(yd\) each doubled) gives a loopless multigraph on \(a,b,c,d,x,y\) with ten edges and degrees \(d(x)=d(y)=5\), \(d(d)=4\), \(d(a)=d(b)=d(c)=2\), as required.
A third route: Erdős–Gallai The Erdős–Gallai criterion says a non-increasing sequence with even sum is graphical exactly when \(\sum_{i\le k} d_i \le k(k-1)+\sum_{i\gt k}\min(d_i,k)\) for every \(k\). For \(5,5,4,2,2,2\) the inequality holds at \(k=1,2,4,5,6\) but fails at \(k=3\): \[ 5+5+4 \;=\; 14 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad 3\cdot2+\min(2,3)+\min(2,3)+\min(2,3) \;=\; 6+6 \;=\; 12 . \] The three vertices of largest degree want \(14\) edge-endpoints, but they can absorb at most \(6\) among themselves and at most \(3\cdot2=6\) from the rest.
Machine check All \(2^{15}=32\,768\) simple graphs on six labelled vertices were enumerated; none has degree sequence \(2,2,2,4,5,5\). The multigraph above was checked to have the right degrees, and the Erdős–Gallai inequalities were evaluated for all six values of \(k\), failing exactly at \(k=3\).
PROBLEM 11

Eleven vertices and forty-five edges

averaging
Problem

Show that if \(G=(V,E)\) satisfies \(|V|=11\) and \(|E|=45\), then it has a vertex of degree at least 9.

Theorem 11.1 Every graph with \(11\) vertices and \(45\) edges has a vertex of degree at least \(9\).
Proof. By the handshake lemma (Lemma A), \[ \sum_{v\in V} d(v) \;=\; 2|E| \;=\; 2\cdot 45 \;=\; 90 . \] Suppose, for contradiction, that every vertex had degree at most \(8\). Summing this bound over the \(11\) vertices gives \[ 90 \;=\; \sum_{v\in V} d(v) \;\le\; 11\cdot 8 \;=\; 88 , \] which is false. Hence some vertex has degree at least \(9\). □

Equivalently: the average degree is \(90/11 = 8.\overline{18}\), and the maximum of a finite list is at least its average, so \(\Delta(G)\ge \lceil 90/11\rceil = 9\).

Answer

The degrees sum to \(2\cdot 45 = 90\). If all eleven were at most \(8\) the sum could not exceed \(11\cdot 8 = 88 < 90\). So some vertex has degree at least \(9\).

Two remarks The argument uses nothing about simplicity, so it holds for multigraphs as well. And the bound \(9\) cannot be improved: since \(\binom{11}{2}=55\), deleting the ten edges of a Hamiltonian path from \(K_{11}\) leaves a simple graph with \(11\) vertices and exactly \(45\) edges in which every degree is \(10\) minus the path degree, i.e. \(8\) or \(9\). So \(\Delta=9\) is attained and the conclusion "degree at least \(10\)" would be false.
PROBLEM 12

The cycle-avoiding game

the game length is an invariant
Problem

Alice and Bob play the following game. Alternately they draw edges between \(n\) given vertices. The player who first obtains a cycle loses the game. Who wins if both players play an optimal strategy, the one who moves first, or the other one?

The surprise is that neither player has a choice that matters. Whatever they do, the game lasts exactly \(n-1\) safe moves and the \(n\)-th move loses; so the winner is decided by the parity of \(n\) before a single edge is drawn.

Theorem 12.1 For \(n\ge2\) the first player wins if \(n\) is even, and the second player wins if \(n\) is odd.
Proof. At every moment the drawn edges form a graph \(F\) on the \(n\) vertices, and as long as nobody has lost, \(F\) is acyclic — a forest. We make two observations.

(i) A safe move exists precisely while \(|E(F)| \lt n-1\). By Lemma D a forest on \(n\) vertices with \(c\) components has \(n-c\) edges. So \(|E(F)| \lt n-1\) means \(c\ge2\): there are two different components, and joining a vertex of one to a vertex of the other creates no cycle (a cycle through the new edge would give a second connection between the two components). That move is safe. Conversely if \(|E(F)| = n-1\) then \(c=1\), so \(F\) is a spanning tree, and every further edge \(uv\) closes a cycle, namely the new edge together with the unique \(u\)–\(v\) path in \(F\).

(ii) The game therefore lasts exactly \(n-1\) safe moves. A player who draws a cycle loses at once, so under optimal play nobody does so voluntarily; and by (i) a safe move is available as long as fewer than \(n-1\) edges are down. After \(n-1\) safe moves the picture is a spanning tree, and by (i) the player to move must create a cycle. There is such an edge to draw, since \(\binom n2-(n-1)=\binom{n-1}{2}\ge 1\) for \(n\ge3\), so that player moves and loses. (For \(n=2\) the single edge is already drawn and the second player has no move at all; with the usual convention that a player unable to move loses, the count below is unchanged.)

(iii) Parity decides. Moves \(1,3,5,\dots\) belong to the first player and moves \(2,4,6,\dots\) to the second. The losing move is move number \(n\). So the first player loses exactly when \(n\) is odd, and the second player loses exactly when \(n\) is even.

□
Answer

The first player wins when \(n\) is even; the second player wins when \(n\) is odd.

Neither player can influence the outcome. A drawn position is always a forest, a safe move exists exactly while the forest has fewer than \(n-1\) edges, and any maximal forest is a spanning tree with exactly \(n-1\) edges. So the game always consists of \(n-1\) safe moves followed by a losing \(n\)-th move, and move \(n\) belongs to the first player precisely when \(n\) is odd.

Machine check The complete game tree was searched for \(n=2,\dots,7\), with both players playing optimally. The winner is the first player for \(n=2,4,6\) and the second player for \(n=3,5,7\) — exactly the parity rule.
PROBLEM 13

One hundred vertices, one of degree 66

component sizes
Problem

Let us assume that simple graph \(G\) has 100 vertices and each has degree at least 33, furthermore, there is a vertex of \(G\) that is incident with at least 66 edges. Prove that \(G\) is connected. (Midterm '15)

Theorem 13.1 Let \(G\) be a simple graph on \(100\) vertices with \(\delta(G)\ge33\), and suppose some vertex \(v_0\) satisfies \(d(v_0)\ge66\). Then \(G\) is connected.
Proof. Suppose \(G\) were disconnected, and let \(H_0\) be the component containing \(v_0\) and \(H_1\) any other component; they are vertex‑disjoint.

By Lemma B a component containing a vertex of degree \(d\) has at least \(d+1\) vertices — that vertex together with its \(d\) neighbours, all of which lie in the same component. Applying this twice: \[ |V(H_0)| \;\ge\; d(v_0)+1 \;\ge\; 67, \qquad\qquad |V(H_1)| \;\ge\; \delta(G)+1 \;\ge\; 34 . \] Since \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) are disjoint subsets of \(V(G)\), \[ 100 \;=\; |V(G)| \;\ge\; |V(H_0)|+|V(H_1)| \;\ge\; 67+34 \;=\; 101 , \] a contradiction. Hence \(G\) has only one component. □

Answer

\(G\) is connected. A component containing a vertex of degree \(d\) has at least \(d+1\) vertices. So the component of \(v_0\) has at least \(67\) vertices and every other component at least \(\delta+1 = 34\); two of them would need \(67+34 = 101 \gt 100\) vertices.

Both hypotheses are needed Neither alone suffices. With \(\delta\ge33\) but no high‑degree vertex, two disjoint copies of \(K_{50}\) give a disconnected graph of minimum degree \(49\ge33\). With a vertex of degree \(66\) but no lower bound elsewhere, a star \(K_{1,66}\) together with \(33\) isolated vertices is disconnected. It is the combination \(67+34\gt100\) that does the work — and it is tight: had the hypothesis read "degree at least \(65\)", the numbers would give \(66+34=100\), and indeed \(K_{66}\,\dot\cup\,K_{34}\) is a disconnected graph with \(\delta=33\) and a vertex of degree \(65\).
Machine check The scaled‑down analogue — \(n=7\), \(\delta\ge2\), some vertex of degree \(\ge4\), where \(5+3=8\gt7\) plays the role of \(67+34\gt100\) — was verified over all graphs on seven vertices: every graph satisfying the hypothesis is connected.
PROBLEM 14

Ten deletions and thirty-three additions

cycle rank and component count
Problem

How many edges does the 66-vertex graph \(G\) have that satisfies the following two properties? (a) At least 10 edges must be deleted from \(G\) in order to obtain a cycle-free graph. (b) At least 33 edges must be added to \(G\) in order to obtain a connected graph.

Read the two conditions as they are meant: \(10\) is the minimum number of deletions that makes \(G\) acyclic, and \(33\) the minimum number of additions that makes it connected. Both minima have closed formulas in \(n\), \(m\) and \(c=c(G)\), and together they pin \(m\) down.

Theorem 14.1 For any graph \(G\) with \(n\) vertices, \(m\) edges and \(c\) components:
(i) the minimum number of edges whose deletion makes \(G\) acyclic is \(m-n+c\);
(ii) the minimum number of edges whose addition makes \(G\) connected is \(c-1\).
Proof of (i). Choose a spanning forest \(F\subseteq G\) — a spanning tree in each component. By Lemma D it has \(n-c\) edges, so deleting the other \(m-(n-c)\) edges leaves the acyclic graph \(F\): that many deletions suffice. Conversely, suppose deleting a set \(D\) leaves \(G-D\) acyclic. Deleting edges never merges components, so \(c(G-D)\ge c\), and by Lemma D the forest \(G-D\) has \(n-c(G-D)\le n-c\) edges. Hence \(|D| = m-|E(G-D)| \ge m-(n-c)\). □
Proof of (ii). By Lemma C each added edge lowers the number of components by at most one, so getting from \(c\) components to \(1\) needs at least \(c-1\) additions. They also suffice: pick one vertex from each component and join them consecutively by \(c-1\) new edges. □

Solving for \(m\)

Here \(n=66\). Condition (b) and Theorem 14.1(ii) give

\[ c-1 = 33 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c = 34 . \]

Condition (a) and Theorem 14.1(i) then give

\[ m-n+c = 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m = n-c+10 = 66-34+10 = \boxed{42} . \]
Answer

\(G\) has 42 edges. The minimum number of deletions making a graph acyclic is \(m-n+c\) and the minimum number of additions making it connected is \(c-1\). So \(c-1=33\) gives \(c=34\), and \(m-66+34=10\) gives \(m=42\).

Such a graph exists Take a path on \(33\) vertices (\(32\) edges), add ten chords from its first vertex, and throw in \(33\) isolated vertices. That is \(66\) vertices, \(42\) edges and \(34\) components; its spanning forest has \(66-34=32\) edges, so \(42-32=10\) deletions are needed to make it acyclic, and \(34-1=33\) additions to connect it. Both were confirmed by brute‑force minimisation.
A word on the wording Taken hyper-literally, "at least 10 must be deleted" and "at least 33 must be added" would only say \(m-n+c\ge10\) and \(c-1\ge33\), which do not determine \(m\) — for instance \(66\) vertices in \(34\) components with a \(K_{33}\) among them satisfies both and has \(528\) edges. The question asks for the number of edges, so the two numbers are the exact minima, as used above.
Machine check Both formulas of Theorem 14.1 were checked against brute‑force minimisation on \(120\) random graphs with up to seven vertices, and the witness graph above was verified to have \(66\) vertices, \(42\) edges, \(34\) components, deletion number \(10\) and addition number \(33\).
PROBLEM 15

All 5-vertex graphs with 2, 8 and 3 edges

exhaustive lists
Problem

Find (up to isomorphism) all \(n\)-vertex \(m\)-edge graphs for  a) \(n=5,\ m=2\);  b) \(n=5,\ m=8\);  c) \(n=5,\ m=3\).

Throughout, isolated vertices are part of the graph — all five vertices are always present. A convenient way to organise the search is by the edge structure: discard the isolated vertices, list the possible graphs with the prescribed number of edges and no isolated vertex, and then pad back up to five vertices whenever there is room.

a) \(n=5\), \(m=2\)

Two edges either share an endvertex or they do not, and there is no third possibility.

  • They share a vertex: the two edges form a path \(P_3\) on three vertices, leaving two isolated vertices — the graph \(P_3\,\dot\cup\,2K_1\), with degree sequence \(2,1,1,0,0\).
  • They are disjoint: they occupy four vertices, leaving one isolated — the graph \(2K_2\,\dot\cup\,K_1\), with degree sequence \(1,1,1,1,0\).

Both fit inside five vertices, and their degree sequences differ, so they are not isomorphic. There are exactly two.

c) \(n=5\), \(m=3\)

We take (c) before (b), because (b) will be read off from (a). Sort the three edges by how their components split.

  • All three edges in one component. A connected graph with three edges has at most four vertices. On three vertices it must be the triangle \(K_3\); on four vertices it has \(4-1=3\) edges, so it is a tree, and the trees on four vertices are the path \(P_4\) and the star \(K_{1,3}\). This gives \(K_3\,\dot\cup\,2K_1\) (degrees \(2,2,2,0,0\)), \(P_4\,\dot\cup\,K_1\) (degrees \(2,2,1,1,0\)) and \(K_{1,3}\,\dot\cup\,K_1\) (degrees \(3,1,1,1,0\)).
  • Split \(2+1\). The two-edge part must be \(P_3\) (three vertices, as disjoint edges would already use four) and the single edge \(K_2\) two more: five vertices exactly, giving \(P_3\,\dot\cup\,K_2\) with degrees \(2,1,1,1,1\).
  • Split \(1+1+1\). Three pairwise disjoint edges need six vertices — impossible here.

The four degree sequences \(2,2,2,0,0\); \(2,2,1,1,0\); \(3,1,1,1,0\); \(2,1,1,1,1\) are pairwise different, so these four graphs are pairwise non-isomorphic. There are exactly four.

b) \(n=5\), \(m=8\)

Here the complement does the work. On five vertices there are \(\binom52 = 10\) possible edges, so \(|E(\overline{G})| = 10-8 = 2\). Complementation is an involution and preserves isomorphism in both directions (\(G_1\cong G_2 \iff \overline{G_1}\cong\overline{G_2}\), because a bijection preserves adjacency exactly when it preserves non-adjacency). Hence

\[ G \;\longmapsto\; \overline{G} \]

is a bijection between the \(5\)-vertex graphs with \(8\) edges and those with \(2\) edges, taken up to isomorphism. By part (a) there are exactly two, namely the complements of the two graphs found there:

  • \(\overline{P_3\,\dot\cup\,2K_1} = K_5 - P_3\): remove two adjacent edges from \(K_5\); degrees \(4,4,3,3,2\).
  • \(\overline{2K_2\,\dot\cup\,K_1} = K_5 - 2K_2\): remove two disjoint edges from \(K_5\); degrees \(4,3,3,3,3\).
Answer

a) two graphs — \(P_3\,\dot\cup\,2K_1\) and \(2K_2\,\dot\cup\,K_1\).
b) two graphs — \(K_5-P_3\) and \(K_5-2K_2\), the complements of those in (a).
c) four graphs — \(K_3\,\dot\cup\,2K_1\), \(P_4\,\dot\cup\,K_1\), \(K_{1,3}\,\dot\cup\,K_1\) and \(P_3\,\dot\cup\,K_2\).

Machine check All \(2^{10}=1024\) labelled graphs on five vertices were sorted into isomorphism classes. The number of classes with \(m\) edges is \(1,1,2,4,6,6,6,4,2,1,1\) for \(m=0,1,\dots,10\) — palindromic, as complementation requires. The entries at \(m=2,3,8\) are \(2,4,2\), and the classes found by machine are exactly the ones listed above.
PROBLEM 16

Graphs isomorphic to their complements

a parity obstruction
Problem

Are there 5-vertex graphs that are isomorphic to their complements? How about 6-vertex graphs?

A graph with \(G\cong\overline{G}\) is called self-complementary. There is one immediate necessary condition.

Lemma 16.1 If \(G\) is a self-complementary graph on \(n\) vertices then \(\binom n2\) is even, i.e. \(n\equiv0\) or \(1 \pmod 4\).
Proof. Every pair of distinct vertices is an edge of exactly one of \(G\) and \(\overline{G}\), so \(|E(G)|+|E(\overline{G})| = \binom n2\). An isomorphism is a bijection on vertices carrying edges onto edges, so isomorphic graphs have equally many edges: \(|E(G)|=|E(\overline{G})|\). Hence \(\binom n2 = 2|E(G)|\) is even. Since \(\binom n2 = n(n-1)/2\), evenness is exactly \(n\equiv 0,1\pmod 4\). □

Five vertices: yes

Here \(\binom52=10\) is even, so the obstruction does not apply — and indeed an example exists. Take the five-cycle \(C_5\) on \(\{0,1,2,3,4\}\) with edges \(01,12,23,34,40\). Its complement has the five edges \(02,13,24,30,41\), which form the closed walk

\[ 0 \;-\; 2 \;-\; 4 \;-\; 1 \;-\; 3 \;-\; 0, \]

a five-cycle again. Explicitly, \(\varphi(i)=2i \bmod 5\) is a bijection of \(\{0,\dots,4\}\) carrying the edges of \(C_5\) onto the edges of \(\overline{C_5}\): a difference of \(\pm1\) is multiplied into \(\pm2\), and the edges of \(\overline{C_5}\) are exactly the pairs at difference \(\pm2\). So \(C_5\cong\overline{C_5}\).

Six vertices: no

Here \(\binom62 = 15\) is odd, so by Lemma 16.1 there is no self-complementary graph on six vertices. Said directly: \(|E(G)| = |E(\overline{G})|\) would force \(15 = 2|E(G)|\), and \(15\) is not even.

Answer

Five vertices: yes. \(C_5\) is isomorphic to its complement (which is again a 5-cycle); there are exactly two such graphs on five vertices.

"No three pairwise non-adjacent vertices in \(G\)" says \(\overline{G}\) has no triangle, and \(\Delta(G)\le5\) gives \(\delta(\overline{G}) \ge 12-5 = 7\). But in a triangle-free graph adjacent vertices have no common neighbour, so for any edge \(uv\) of \(\overline{G}\) the sets \(N(u)\) and \(N(v)\) are disjoint subsets of the \(13\) vertices:

\[ 14 \;\le\; d_{\overline{G}}(u)+d_{\overline{G}}(v) \;=\; |N(u)\cup N(v)| \;\le\; 13 , \]

a contradiction. (There is an edge to apply this to, since \(\delta(\overline{G})\ge7\gt0\).)

Answer

Suppose nobody knows six others. Take any person \(v\): they know at most \(5\), so at least \(13-1-5=7\) people are strangers to \(v\). If two of those seven are strangers to each other, they and \(v\) are three mutual strangers. Otherwise those seven all know one another, and then each of them knows at least \(6\) people — somebody knows at least six. Either way the claim holds.

Thirteen is sharp With twelve people the statement fails. Split them into two groups of six and let acquaintance mean "same group": each person then knows exactly \(5\) others, and any three people include two from the same group, who know each other. In graph terms \(G = K_6\,\dot\cup\,K_6\) is \(5\)-regular on \(12\) vertices with no three pairwise non-adjacent vertices. So the count \(13-1-5=7\) is exactly what makes the argument run.
Machine check The triangle-free inequality \(d(u)+d(v)\le n\) was verified over all graphs on at most seven vertices; the \(12\)-vertex example was checked to be \(5\)-regular with no independent set of size \(3\); and \(200\,000\) random graphs on \(13\) vertices with maximum degree \(\le5\) were searched — every one contains three pairwise non-adjacent vertices.
PROBLEM 26

Tiling a rectangle by integer-sided rectangles

double counting corners
Problem

Show that if a rectangle \(T\) is tiled by rectangles so that each tile has at least one side of integer length, then \(T\) also has a side of integer length.

Place \(T\) with its lower-left corner at the origin, so \(T = [0,w]\times[0,h]\), and call a point of the plane integral if both of its coordinates are integers. Everything follows from counting, in two ways, the pairs (tile, integral corner of that tile) — the same double count that proves the handshake lemma.

Lemma 26.1 A rectangle with a side of integer length has an even number of integral corners.
Proof. Let \(R = [a,b]\times[c,d]\) and suppose its width \(b-a\) is an integer (the case of integer height is identical with the roles of the axes exchanged). Then \(a\in\mathbb{Z} \iff b\in\mathbb{Z}\). The four corners are \((a,c),(b,c),(a,d),(b,d)\), and \[ (a,c) \text{ integral} \iff a,c\in\mathbb{Z} \iff b,c\in\mathbb{Z} \iff (b,c) \text{ integral}, \] so the bottom two corners are integral together or not at all; likewise the top two. The count is thus \(0\), \(2\) or \(4\). □
Lemma 26.2 For a point \(p\) let \(t(p)\) be the number of tiles having \(p\) as a corner. Then \(t(p)=1\) if \(p\) is a corner of \(T\), and \(t(p)\) is even for every other point \(p\).
Proof. Take \(\delta\gt0\) so small that the only tile boundaries meeting the \(\delta\)-disc around \(p\) are those through \(p\) itself; then each of the four small quadrants at \(p\) that lies inside \(T\) is covered by a single tile. A tile \(A\) covering, say, the north-east quadrant has \(p\) as a corner exactly when it does not extend west or south of \(p\) — that is, exactly when \(A\) differs from the tiles covering the north-west and the south-east quadrants.
  • \(p\) interior to \(T\): all four quadrants are present. If one tile covers all four, \(t(p)=0\). If two tiles split the disc along a line through \(p\), each contains \(p\) in the interior of a side and \(t(p)=0\). At a T-junction three tiles meet and exactly the two on the stem side have a corner at \(p\), so \(t(p)=2\). If four distinct tiles meet, each has a corner at \(p\) and \(t(p)=4\). (Three quadrants cannot share one tile: an axis-parallel rectangle containing points of the north-east and south-west quadrants contains the whole box between them, hence all four.) In every case \(t(p)\) is even.
  • \(p\) on a side of \(T\) but not a corner: only two quadrants lie inside \(T\), covered by tiles \(A\) and \(B\). If \(A=B\) then \(p\) is interior to a side of it and \(t(p)=0\); if \(A\ne B\) then both have a corner at \(p\) and \(t(p)=2\).
  • \(p\) a corner of \(T\): one quadrant, one tile, and its corner is \(p\). So \(t(p)=1\).
□
Theorem 26.3 If every tile has a side of integer length, then \(T\) has a side of integer length.
Proof. Count the pairs \((R,p)\) where \(R\) is a tile and \(p\) an integral corner of \(R\); call the total \(N\).

By tiles. Every tile has an integer side, so by Lemma 26.1 contributes an even number. Hence \(N\) is even.

By points. Grouping instead by the integral point \(p\), \[ N \;=\; \sum_{p \text{ integral}} t(p) . \] By Lemma 26.2 every term is even except those coming from integral corners of \(T\), each of which contributes exactly \(1\). Therefore \[ N \;\equiv\; \#\{\text{integral corners of } T\} \pmod 2 . \]

Since \(N\) is even, \(T\) has an even number of integral corners. But suppose neither \(w\) nor \(h\) were an integer. The corners of \(T\) are \((0,0),(w,0),(0,h),(w,h)\); the first is integral, while \((w,0)\) and \((w,h)\) fail because \(w\notin\mathbb{Z}\) and \((0,h)\) fails because \(h\notin\mathbb{Z}\). That is exactly one integral corner — an odd number, a contradiction.

Hence \(w\in\mathbb{Z}\) or \(h\in\mathbb{Z}\). □

Answer

Put \(T=[0,w]\times[0,h]\) and call a point integral when both coordinates are integers. Count the pairs (tile, integral corner of it) two ways. By tiles: a rectangle with an integer side has \(0\), \(2\) or \(4\) integral corners, so the total is even. By points: the number of tiles with a corner at a given point is even everywhere except at the four corners of \(T\), where it is \(1\). So \(T\) must have an even number of integral corners — whereas a rectangle with neither side an integer has exactly one, namely the origin. Hence \(w\) or \(h\) is an integer.

Machine check Randomly generated tilings whose every tile has an integer side — \(2\,220\) of them, with up to \(33\) tiles — were tested: every tile had an even number of integral corners, every container had an integer side, and across \(5\,737\) corner points the count \(t(p)\) was \(1\) at each corner of the container and even everywhere else, exactly as Lemma 26.2 asserts.

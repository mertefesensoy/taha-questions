PROBLEM 21

Every graph has an acyclic orientation

order the vertices
Problem

Prove that the edges of a simple graph \(G=(V,E)\) can be oriented so that the directed graph obtained does not contain a directed cycle.

Theorem 21.1 Every finite simple graph admits an acyclic orientation.
Proof. Since \(V\) is finite, we may number its vertices \(v_1,v_2,\dots,v_n\); write \(\nu(v_i)=i\) for the label of a vertex. Orient every edge from its endvertex of smaller label to its endvertex of larger label: \[ \text{for } e=\{v_i,v_j\}\in E \text{ with } i \lt j, \text{ orient } e \text{ as } v_i \to v_j . \] This is well defined: \(G\) is simple, so an edge has two distinct endvertices and their labels differ.

Suppose the resulting digraph contained a directed cycle \(u_1 \to u_2 \to \dots \to u_k \to u_1\) with \(k\ge2\). Each arc goes from a smaller label to a larger one, so \[ \nu(u_1) \;\lt\; \nu(u_2) \;\lt\; \dots \;\lt\; \nu(u_k) \;\lt\; \nu(u_1) , \] where the last inequality comes from the closing arc \(u_k\to u_1\). Chaining them gives \(\nu(u_1)\lt\nu(u_1)\), which is false. Hence no directed cycle exists. □

Answer

Number the vertices \(1,\dots,n\) and orient each edge from the smaller label to the larger. Along any directed walk the labels strictly increase, so a directed cycle would return to its start with a strictly larger label than it left with — impossible. The orientation is therefore acyclic.

Remarks The proof uses only that \(V\) carries a linear order, so it works for any graph whose vertex set can be linearly ordered — every finite graph, and with the axiom of choice every infinite one too. Conversely, every acyclic orientation arises this way: an acyclic digraph has a topological order, and orienting by that order reproduces it. Simplicity matters only to exclude loops, which could never be oriented acyclically. Finally, the number of acyclic orientations of \(G\) is \(|\chi_G(-1)|\), where \(\chi_G\) is the chromatic polynomial (Stanley's theorem) — far more than we need, but it shows these orientations are plentiful, not exceptional.
Machine check Three thousand random simple graphs on up to seven vertices were oriented by the rule above; a depth-first search found a directed cycle in none of them.
PROBLEM 22

Three edges, three cuts

bridges after a deletion
Problem

Let \(e\), \(f\) and \(g\) be edges of simple, connected graph \(G\). Assume that \(G\) remains connected if any one of its edges is deleted, however neither \(G-e-g\) nor \(G-f-g\) is connected. Prove that graph \(G-f-g\) is also disconnected.

Reading the statement As printed, the conclusion "\(G-f-g\) is disconnected" is already one of the hypotheses, so it cannot be what is meant. The hypotheses concern the pairs \(\{e,g\}\) and \(\{f,g\}\); the conclusion must therefore concern the third pair, \(\{e,f\}\). We prove that \(G-e-f\) is disconnected, and take \(e,f,g\) to be pairwise distinct, as the wording implies. (Some distinctness is genuinely needed: for \(e=f\) the claim would read "\(G-e\) is disconnected", contradicting the hypothesis that \(G\) survives any single deletion.)
Theorem 22.1 Let \(G\) be connected with no bridge, and let \(e,f,g\) be three distinct edges such that \(G-e-g\) and \(G-f-g\) are both disconnected. Then \(G-e-f\) is disconnected.
Proof. Put \(H := G-g\).

Step 1: \(H\) is connected, and both \(e\) and \(f\) are bridges of \(H\). By hypothesis \(G\) stays connected after the deletion of any single edge, so \(H=G-g\) is connected. Also \(H-e = G-e-g\) is disconnected, so the edge \(e\) is a bridge of \(H\); likewise \(H-f = G-f-g\) is disconnected, so \(f\) is a bridge of \(H\).

Step 2: \(H-e-f\) has exactly three components. Deleting the bridge \(e\) from the connected graph \(H\) leaves exactly two components (by Lemma C read backwards: \(e\) lies on no cycle of \(H\), so \(c(H) = c(H-e)-1\), giving \(c(H-e)=2\)). The edge \(f\ne e\) survives in \(H-e\) and lies inside one of those two components; \(f\) is a bridge of \(H\), hence also of \(H-e\) — deleting edges cannot create a cycle through \(f\) — so deleting it splits that component in two. Therefore \[ c(H-e-f) \;=\; 3 . \]

Step 3: putting \(g\) back. Now \(G-e-f\) is exactly \(H-e-f\) with the single edge \(g\) restored. By Lemma C one edge lowers the number of components by at most one, so \[ c(G-e-f) \;=\; c\bigl((H-e-f)+g\bigr) \;\ge\; 3-1 \;=\; 2 . \] A graph with at least two components is disconnected. □

Answer

\(G-e-f\) is disconnected. Since \(G\) is bridgeless, \(H=G-g\) is connected; and since \(G-e-g\) and \(G-f-g\) are disconnected, \(e\) and \(f\) are both bridges of \(H\). Deleting two distinct bridges from a connected graph leaves three components, and restoring the single edge \(g\) can rejoin at most two of them — so \(G-e-f\) has at least two components.

The same statement in cut language Because \(G\) is bridgeless while \(G-e-g\) is disconnected, the pair \(\{e,g\}\) is a minimal edge cut; likewise \(\{f,g\}\). The cuts of a graph form a vector space over \(\mathbb{F}_2\) under symmetric difference, and \[ \{e,g\}\;\triangle\;\{f,g\} \;=\; \{e,f\}, \] so \(\{e,f\}\) is a cut too — which is exactly the conclusion. The elementary argument above avoids setting up the cut space, but this is what makes the result feel inevitable.
Machine check Every connected bridgeless graph on at most six vertices was enumerated together with every ordered triple of distinct edges \((e,f,g)\). In all \(35\,898\) instances satisfying the hypotheses, \(G-e-f\) was indeed disconnected.
PROBLEM 23

Deleting a column keeps the rows distinct

a graph on the rows
Problem

All rows of an \(n\times n\) table \(T\) are pairwise distinct. Prove that \(T\) has a column such that the table obtained by the deletion of that column still has pairwise distinct rows.

There is nothing graph-like in the statement, so we build the graph ourselves — on the rows — and then count edges against vertices using problem 17.

Definition Call column \(j\) critical if deleting it destroys distinctness, i.e. if some two rows of \(T\) agree in every column except \(j\). The claim to prove is that not all \(n\) columns are critical.
Lemma 23.1 If rows \(r\ne s\) agree in every column except \(j\), then \(j\) is determined by the pair \(\{r,s\}\).
Proof. Rows \(r\) and \(s\) are distinct, so they differ somewhere; agreeing outside \(j\) means they differ exactly in column \(j\). If they also differed exactly in column \(j'\) with \(j'\ne j\), then column \(j\) would be one of the columns "outside \(j'\)", where they agree — contradicting that they differ in column \(j\). □
Theorem 23.2 Some column of \(T\) may be deleted without making two rows equal.
Proof. Suppose every one of the \(n\) columns is critical. Build a graph \(\Gamma\):
  • the \(n\) vertices of \(\Gamma\) are the \(n\) rows of \(T\);
  • for each column \(j\), choose one pair of rows \(\{r_j,s_j\}\) agreeing outside column \(j\) (such a pair exists, as \(j\) is critical) and put the edge \(r_js_j\) into \(\Gamma\), labelling it with \(j\).
By Lemma 23.1 different columns yield different pairs, so these \(n\) edges are distinct: \(\Gamma\) has \(n\) vertices and \(n\) edges. By Theorem 17.2, \(\Gamma\) contains a cycle \[ r_1 \;\overset{j_1}{-}\; r_2 \;\overset{j_2}{-}\; \cdots \;\overset{j_{k-1}}{-}\; r_k \;\overset{j_k}{-}\; r_1 , \qquad k\ge3, \] whose edge labels \(j_1,\dots,j_k\) are pairwise distinct columns.

Now look at what happens in column \(j_1\). The edge \(r_1r_2\) is labelled \(j_1\), so \(r_1\) and \(r_2\) differ in column \(j_1\). Every other edge of the cycle carries a label \(j_i \ne j_1\), so its two rows agree in column \(j_1\). Walking the cycle the long way round, \[ r_2,\; r_3,\; \dots,\; r_k,\; r_1 \] are consecutive along edges labelled \(j_2,\dots,j_k\), all different from \(j_1\); hence all of these rows agree with one another in column \(j_1\). In particular \(r_2\) and \(r_1\) agree in column \(j_1\) — contradicting that they differ there.

So not every column is critical, and any non-critical column may be deleted.

□
Answer

Call a column critical if deleting it makes two rows equal, and suppose all \(n\) were. Form a graph on the \(n\) rows by joining, for each column \(j\), a pair of rows that agree everywhere except in column \(j\); different columns give different pairs, so this graph has \(n\) vertices and \(n\) edges and therefore contains a cycle. Along that cycle every edge but one keeps the entry in the first edge's column constant, forcing its two endpoints to agree in that column — while that edge says they differ. Hence some column is not critical and can be deleted.

Machine check All \(n\times n\) tables with pairwise distinct rows were enumerated for \(n=2,3\) over a two-letter alphabet and for \(n=3\) over a three-letter alphabet — \(17\,898\) tables in total. Every one of them has a deletable column.

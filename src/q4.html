PROBLEM 18

Add one edge, delete two

two competing budgets
Problem

Is it possible to obtain a connected graph from a graph of 88 vertices, 42 components and 111 edges by a sequence of steps, where one step consists of adding one edge and deleting two edges at the same time? (An edge deleted earlier can be added later.)

Two quantities are worth watching: the number of edges, which the step controls exactly, and the number of components, which it can only move slowly. They pull in opposite directions.

Theorem 18.1 No such sequence of steps reaches a connected graph.
Proof. Write \(n=88\), and let \(m_k\) and \(c_k\) be the number of edges and of components after \(k\) steps, so \(m_0=111\) and \(c_0=42\).

The edge budget. Each step adds one edge and deletes two, so \(m_k = 111-k\). By Theorem 17.1 a connected graph on \(88\) vertices needs at least \(87\) edges. So if the graph is connected after \(k\) steps then \[ 111-k \;\ge\; 87 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k \;\le\; 24 . \]

The component budget. Within one step, adding an edge lowers the number of components by at most one (Lemma C), while deleting edges can only raise it or leave it unchanged — deleting an edge never merges two components. Hence \(c_k \ge c_{k-1}-1\) for every \(k\), regardless of the order in which the three edges are processed, and therefore \[ c_k \;\ge\; 42-k . \] Being connected means \(c_k=1\), which forces \(1\ge 42-k\), i.e. \(k\ge 41\).

The two requirements are incompatible: \(41 \le k \le 24\) is empty. So no sequence of steps produces a connected graph. □

Answer

No, it is impossible. Each step costs one edge net, so after \(k\) steps there are \(111-k\) edges; connectivity on \(88\) vertices needs \(87\) of them, giving \(k\le24\). But each step can lower the number of components by at most one, so getting from \(42\) components down to \(1\) needs \(k\ge41\). Since \(41\gt24\), the target is unreachable.

How close is it? Not close. Even spending the entire edge budget — \(24\) steps, ending at exactly \(87\) edges, the bare minimum for a spanning tree — removes at most \(24\) of the \(41\) components that must go. The obstruction is not a parity accident but a shortfall of \(17\) steps.
PROBLEM 19

Delete one edge, add two

the cycle rank as a monovariant
Problem

Is it possible to obtain a cycle-free simple graph from a 77-vertex simple graph of 66 edges and 42 components by applying a sequence of steps where a step consists of deletion of one edge and addition of two edges? (An edge deleted earlier is possible to be added back later.)

Now the edge count moves the other way, and the right quantity to track is the one that measures "how far from cycle-free" the graph is.

Definition The cycle rank of a graph is \(\rho(G) = m-n+c\), where \(m\), \(n\), \(c\) are its numbers of edges, vertices and components. By Theorem 17.1 we have \(\rho(G)\ge0\), and by Lemma D, \(\rho(G)=0\) exactly when \(G\) is cycle-free.
Lemma 19.1 Deleting one edge lowers \(\rho\) by at most \(1\); adding one edge never lowers \(\rho\) at all.
Proof. Delete an edge \(e\): then \(m\) drops by \(1\), and \(c\) either stays (if \(e\) lay on a cycle) or rises by \(1\) (if \(e\) was a bridge). So \(\rho\) changes by \(-1+0=-1\) or by \(-1+1=0\). Add an edge \(e\): by Lemma C, \(m\) rises by \(1\) and \(c\) either falls by \(1\) (no cycle through \(e\)) or stays (a cycle through \(e\)). So \(\rho\) changes by \(+1-1=0\) or by \(+1-0=+1\). □
Theorem 19.2 No such sequence of steps reaches a cycle-free graph.
Proof. At the start \(n=77\), \(m_0=66\), \(c_0=42\), so \[ \rho_0 \;=\; 66-77+42 \;=\; 31 . \]

The rank budget. One step deletes one edge and adds two, so by Lemma 19.1 it lowers \(\rho\) by at most \(1\) in total. Reaching a cycle-free graph means \(\rho=0\), so at least \(31\) steps are required: \(k\ge31\).

The edge budget. Each step raises the edge count by one, so after \(k\) steps there are \(66+k\) edges. A cycle-free graph on \(77\) vertices is a forest, and by Lemma D it has at most \(76\) edges. So \[ 66+k \;\le\; 76 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad k \;\le\; 10 . \]

Again \(31\le k\le10\) is impossible. □

Answer

No, it is impossible. The cycle rank \(m-n+c\) starts at \(66-77+42=31\) and must reach \(0\); one step (delete one edge, add two) lowers it by at most \(1\), so at least \(31\) steps are needed. But each step adds a net edge, and a forest on \(77\) vertices has at most \(76\) edges, allowing at most \(10\) steps.

Machine check Lemma 19.1 was tested on \(4000\) random steps applied to random graphs on up to seven vertices: in every case the cycle rank dropped by at most one.
PROBLEM 20

\(2k\) vertices, minimum degree \(k-1\)

component sizes again
Problem

Simple graph \(G\) has \(2k\) vertices. Every vertex has degree at least \(k-1\), and there exists at least one vertex of degree \(k\). Show that \(G\) is connected.

Theorem 20.1 Let \(G\) be a simple graph on \(n=2k\) vertices with \(\delta(G)\ge k-1\), and suppose some vertex \(v_0\) has \(d(v_0)\ge k\). Then \(G\) is connected.
Proof. Suppose \(G\) is disconnected, and let \(H_0\) be the component containing \(v_0\) and \(H_1\) a different component. By Lemma B, a component containing a vertex of degree \(d\) has at least \(d+1\) vertices, so \[ |V(H_0)| \;\ge\; d(v_0)+1 \;\ge\; k+1, \qquad\qquad |V(H_1)| \;\ge\; \delta(G)+1 \;\ge\; k . \] The two components are disjoint, so \[ 2k \;=\; |V(G)| \;\ge\; |V(H_0)|+|V(H_1)| \;\ge\; (k+1)+k \;=\; 2k+1 , \] which is absurd. Hence \(G\) has a single component. □
Answer

\(G\) is connected. The component of the degree-\(k\) vertex holds at least \(k+1\) vertices and any other component at least \((k-1)+1 = k\); two of them would need \(2k+1\) vertices, one more than \(G\) has.

The extra vertex is indispensable Dropping the vertex of degree \(k\) makes the statement false: for \(k=3\), two disjoint triangles form a graph on \(2k=6\) vertices with every degree equal to \(k-1=2\), and it is disconnected. In general \(K_k\,\dot\cup\,K_k\) is \((k-1)\)-regular on \(2k\) vertices and disconnected. The single vertex of degree \(k\) is exactly what breaks the tie \(k+k=2k\).
Machine check Exhaustive for \(k=1,2,3\) (that is, \(n=2,4,6\)): among all graphs on those vertex sets, \(12\,037\) satisfy the hypothesis and every one of them is connected. Without the degree-\(k\) requirement, \(10\) disconnected graphs on six vertices satisfy \(\delta\ge2\).
PROBLEM ★

Three colours, 123 edges

three spanning trees
Problem

At most how many vertices may a graph \(G\) have, that has 123 edges and its edges can be coloured using colours red, white and green (every edge gets exactly one colour), so that between any two vertices of \(G\) there exist paths consisting of only red, only white as well as only green edges?

The condition says precisely that each of the three colour classes, taken as a spanning subgraph on all of \(V(G)\), is connected.

Theorem ★.1 Such a graph has at most \(42\) vertices, and \(42\) is attained.
Upper bound. Let \(n=|V(G)|\) and let \(R\), \(W\), \(Z\) be the sets of red, white and green edges; they partition \(E(G)\), so \(|R|+|W|+|Z| = 123\). The hypothesis says that any two vertices are joined by a red path, so the spanning subgraph \((V(G),R)\) is connected; by Theorem 17.1 it has at least \(n-1\) edges, i.e. \(|R|\ge n-1\), and likewise \(|W|\ge n-1\) and \(|Z|\ge n-1\). Adding, \[ 123 \;=\; |R|+|W|+|Z| \;\ge\; 3(n-1) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n-1 \;\le\; 41 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad n \;\le\; 42 . \]□
Attainment. For \(n=42\) equality must hold everywhere, so each colour class must be a connected spanning subgraph with exactly \(41=n-1\) edges — that is, a spanning tree. Three pairwise edge-disjoint spanning trees on \(42\) vertices do exist. Work in \(\mathbb{Z}_{42}\) and, for \(k=0,1,2\), let \(P_k\) be the zig-zag sequence \[ P_k:\quad k,\;\; k+1,\;\; k-1,\;\; k+2,\;\; k-2,\;\;\dots,\;\; k+20,\;\; k-20,\;\; k+21 \pmod{42} . \]

Each \(P_k\) is a Hamiltonian path. Its terms are \(k\), then \(k+i\) and \(k-i\) for \(i=1,\dots,20\), and finally \(k+21\). That is \(1+40+1=42\) terms, and they are pairwise distinct modulo \(42\): the residues \(k, k\pm1,\dots,k\pm20\) are \(41\) distinct values and \(k+21\equiv k-21\) is the one remaining. So \(P_k\) visits every vertex exactly once and is a spanning tree with \(41\) edges.

The three paths are pairwise edge-disjoint. Consecutive terms of \(P_k\) come in just two shapes, \(\{k-(i-1),\,k+i\}\) and \(\{k+i,\,k-i\}\), whose endpoint sums are \[ (k-i+1)+(k+i) \;=\; 2k+1 , \qquad\qquad (k+i)+(k-i) \;=\; 2k \qquad \pmod{42} . \] So every edge of \(P_k\) has endpoint sum \(2k\) or \(2k+1\). For \(k=0,1,2\) these sum sets are \(\{0,1\}\), \(\{2,3\}\) and \(\{4,5\}\), which are pairwise disjoint; an edge belonging to two of the paths would have to have its endpoint sum in two disjoint sets. Hence no edge is shared.

Colouring \(P_0\) red, \(P_1\) white and \(P_2\) green therefore gives a graph on \(42\) vertices with \(3\cdot41 = 123\) edges in which every colour class is a spanning tree, hence connected.

□
Answer

At most 42 vertices, and 42 is achievable. Each colour class must be a connected spanning subgraph, hence has at least \(n-1\) edges, so \(3(n-1)\le123\) and \(n\le42\). At \(n=42\) each class is forced to be a spanning tree, and three edge-disjoint spanning trees on \(42\) vertices exist — for example three of the \(21\) Hamiltonian paths into which \(K_{42}\) decomposes.

Machine check The three zig-zag paths on \(42\) vertices were built explicitly and verified: each visits all \(42\) vertices exactly once, each has \(41\) edges and is connected, the endpoint sums of their edges are exactly \(\{0,1\}\), \(\{2,3\}\) and \(\{4,5\}\) as claimed, they are pairwise edge-disjoint, and their union has exactly \(123\) edges.

This is the companion volume to Degrees, Regularity, Isomorphism and Connectivity, which solves problems 1–8 of the same sheet. Here we treat problems 9–26 together with the starred problem that sits between 20 and 21. Nothing from the first volume is assumed beyond the handshake lemma and the definition of a component, both restated below, so this document can be read on its own.

Every solution is obtained by graph‑theoretic means. Several of the problems are not posed in the language of graphs — a game played with pencil and paper (12), a question about a table of numbers (23), a party (25), a tiled rectangle (26) — and in each the first step is to identify the graph hiding inside. Where a problem asks for a construction or an exhaustive list, the objects are exhibited and checked one by one rather than merely asserted to exist.

All computational claims were additionally confirmed by exhaustive machine search: every graph on at most six or seven vertices where that suffices, the complete game tree for problem 12, brute‑force minimisation for the two extremal quantities of problem 14, and randomised tilings for problem 26. Those checks are reported alongside the arguments; they corroborate the proofs rather than replace them.

What is assumed

Graphs are finite; they are simple unless the problem explicitly allows otherwise. We write \(n=|V(G)|\), \(m=|E(G)|\), \(d(v)\) for the degree of \(v\), \(\Delta(G)\) and \(\delta(G)\) for the largest and smallest degree, and \(c(G)\) for the number of connected components. \(K_n\), \(P_n\) and \(C_n\) are the complete graph, the path and the cycle on \(n\) vertices, and \(\overline{G}\) is the complement of \(G\). Four facts are used throughout.

Lemma A (handshake lemma) \(\sum_{v\in V(G)} d(v) = 2m\).
Proof. Count the pairs \((v,e)\) with \(v\) an endvertex of \(e\) in two ways: by edges, each contributing \(2\); by vertices, vertex \(v\) contributing \(d(v)\). □
Lemma B (components are degree‑closed) If \(H\) is a component of \(G\) and \(x\in V(H)\), then every neighbour of \(x\) lies in \(H\); hence \(d_H(x)=d_G(x)\) and \(|V(H)|\ge d_G(x)+1\).
Proof. A neighbour \(y\) of \(x\) is joined to \(x\) by an edge, so it lies in the same component. The \(d_G(x)\) neighbours together with \(x\) itself are \(d_G(x)+1\) distinct vertices of \(H\). □
Lemma C (edge addition, EAL) Adding one edge \(e\) to \(G\) either creates no cycle through \(e\), in which case \(c(G+e)=c(G)-1\), or creates a cycle through \(e\), in which case \(c(G+e)=c(G)\). In particular one edge lowers the number of components by at most one.
Lemma D (forests) A forest on \(n\) vertices with \(c\) components has exactly \(n-c\) edges. Consequently a forest has at most \(n-1\) edges, with equality exactly for trees.
Proof. Build the forest edge by edge from the edgeless graph, which has \(n\) components. No added edge ever lies on a cycle, so by Lemma C each addition lowers the component count by exactly one. After \(m\) additions the count is \(n-m\), and this equals \(c\). □

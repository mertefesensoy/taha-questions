PROBLEM 12

The cycle-avoiding game

the game length is an invariant
Problem

Alice and Bob play the following game. Alternately they draw edges between \(n\) given vertices. The player who first obtains a cycle loses the game. Who wins if both players play an optimal strategy, the one who moves first, or the other one?

The surprise is that neither player has a choice that matters. Whatever they do, the game lasts exactly \(n-1\) safe moves and the \(n\)-th move loses; so the winner is decided by the parity of \(n\) before a single edge is drawn.

Theorem 12.1 For \(n\ge2\) the first player wins if \(n\) is even, and the second player wins if \(n\) is odd.
Proof. At every moment the drawn edges form a graph \(F\) on the \(n\) vertices, and as long as nobody has lost, \(F\) is acyclic — a forest. We make two observations.

(i) A safe move exists precisely while \(|E(F)| \lt n-1\). By Lemma D a forest on \(n\) vertices with \(c\) components has \(n-c\) edges. So \(|E(F)| \lt n-1\) means \(c\ge2\): there are two different components, and joining a vertex of one to a vertex of the other creates no cycle (a cycle through the new edge would give a second connection between the two components). That move is safe. Conversely if \(|E(F)| = n-1\) then \(c=1\), so \(F\) is a spanning tree, and every further edge \(uv\) closes a cycle, namely the new edge together with the unique \(u\)–\(v\) path in \(F\).

(ii) The game therefore lasts exactly \(n-1\) safe moves. A player who draws a cycle loses at once, so under optimal play nobody does so voluntarily; and by (i) a safe move is available as long as fewer than \(n-1\) edges are down. After \(n-1\) safe moves the picture is a spanning tree, and by (i) the player to move must create a cycle. There is such an edge to draw, since \(\binom n2-(n-1)=\binom{n-1}{2}\ge 1\) for \(n\ge3\), so that player moves and loses. (For \(n=2\) the single edge is already drawn and the second player has no move at all; with the usual convention that a player unable to move loses, the count below is unchanged.)

(iii) Parity decides. Moves \(1,3,5,\dots\) belong to the first player and moves \(2,4,6,\dots\) to the second. The losing move is move number \(n\). So the first player loses exactly when \(n\) is odd, and the second player loses exactly when \(n\) is even.

□
Answer

The first player wins when \(n\) is even; the second player wins when \(n\) is odd.

Neither player can influence the outcome. A drawn position is always a forest, a safe move exists exactly while the forest has fewer than \(n-1\) edges, and any maximal forest is a spanning tree with exactly \(n-1\) edges. So the game always consists of \(n-1\) safe moves followed by a losing \(n\)-th move, and move \(n\) belongs to the first player precisely when \(n\) is odd.

Machine check The complete game tree was searched for \(n=2,\dots,7\), with both players playing optimally. The winner is the first player for \(n=2,4,6\) and the second player for \(n=3,5,7\) — exactly the parity rule.
PROBLEM 13

One hundred vertices, one of degree 66

component sizes
Problem

Let us assume that simple graph \(G\) has 100 vertices and each has degree at least 33, furthermore, there is a vertex of \(G\) that is incident with at least 66 edges. Prove that \(G\) is connected. (Midterm '15)

Theorem 13.1 Let \(G\) be a simple graph on \(100\) vertices with \(\delta(G)\ge33\), and suppose some vertex \(v_0\) satisfies \(d(v_0)\ge66\). Then \(G\) is connected.
Proof. Suppose \(G\) were disconnected, and let \(H_0\) be the component containing \(v_0\) and \(H_1\) any other component; they are vertex‑disjoint.

By Lemma B a component containing a vertex of degree \(d\) has at least \(d+1\) vertices — that vertex together with its \(d\) neighbours, all of which lie in the same component. Applying this twice: \[ |V(H_0)| \;\ge\; d(v_0)+1 \;\ge\; 67, \qquad\qquad |V(H_1)| \;\ge\; \delta(G)+1 \;\ge\; 34 . \] Since \(H_0\) and \(H_1\) are disjoint subsets of \(V(G)\), \[ 100 \;=\; |V(G)| \;\ge\; |V(H_0)|+|V(H_1)| \;\ge\; 67+34 \;=\; 101 , \] a contradiction. Hence \(G\) has only one component. □

Answer

\(G\) is connected. A component containing a vertex of degree \(d\) has at least \(d+1\) vertices. So the component of \(v_0\) has at least \(67\) vertices and every other component at least \(\delta+1 = 34\); two of them would need \(67+34 = 101 \gt 100\) vertices.

Both hypotheses are needed Neither alone suffices. With \(\delta\ge33\) but no high‑degree vertex, two disjoint copies of \(K_{50}\) give a disconnected graph of minimum degree \(49\ge33\). With a vertex of degree \(66\) but no lower bound elsewhere, a star \(K_{1,66}\) together with \(33\) isolated vertices is disconnected. It is the combination \(67+34\gt100\) that does the work — and it is tight: had the hypothesis read "degree at least \(65\)", the numbers would give \(66+34=100\), and indeed \(K_{66}\,\dot\cup\,K_{34}\) is a disconnected graph with \(\delta=33\) and a vertex of degree \(65\).
Machine check The scaled‑down analogue — \(n=7\), \(\delta\ge2\), some vertex of degree \(\ge4\), where \(5+3=8\gt7\) plays the role of \(67+34\gt100\) — was verified over all graphs on seven vertices: every graph satisfying the hypothesis is connected.
PROBLEM 14

Ten deletions and thirty-three additions

cycle rank and component count
Problem

How many edges does the 66-vertex graph \(G\) have that satisfies the following two properties? (a) At least 10 edges must be deleted from \(G\) in order to obtain a cycle-free graph. (b) At least 33 edges must be added to \(G\) in order to obtain a connected graph.

Read the two conditions as they are meant: \(10\) is the minimum number of deletions that makes \(G\) acyclic, and \(33\) the minimum number of additions that makes it connected. Both minima have closed formulas in \(n\), \(m\) and \(c=c(G)\), and together they pin \(m\) down.

Theorem 14.1 For any graph \(G\) with \(n\) vertices, \(m\) edges and \(c\) components:
(i) the minimum number of edges whose deletion makes \(G\) acyclic is \(m-n+c\);
(ii) the minimum number of edges whose addition makes \(G\) connected is \(c-1\).
Proof of (i). Choose a spanning forest \(F\subseteq G\) — a spanning tree in each component. By Lemma D it has \(n-c\) edges, so deleting the other \(m-(n-c)\) edges leaves the acyclic graph \(F\): that many deletions suffice. Conversely, suppose deleting a set \(D\) leaves \(G-D\) acyclic. Deleting edges never merges components, so \(c(G-D)\ge c\), and by Lemma D the forest \(G-D\) has \(n-c(G-D)\le n-c\) edges. Hence \(|D| = m-|E(G-D)| \ge m-(n-c)\). □
Proof of (ii). By Lemma C each added edge lowers the number of components by at most one, so getting from \(c\) components to \(1\) needs at least \(c-1\) additions. They also suffice: pick one vertex from each component and join them consecutively by \(c-1\) new edges. □

Solving for \(m\)

Here \(n=66\). Condition (b) and Theorem 14.1(ii) give

\[ c-1 = 33 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad c = 34 . \]

Condition (a) and Theorem 14.1(i) then give

\[ m-n+c = 10 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad m = n-c+10 = 66-34+10 = \boxed{42} . \]
Answer

\(G\) has 42 edges. The minimum number of deletions making a graph acyclic is \(m-n+c\) and the minimum number of additions making it connected is \(c-1\). So \(c-1=33\) gives \(c=34\), and \(m-66+34=10\) gives \(m=42\).

Such a graph exists Take a path on \(33\) vertices (\(32\) edges), add ten chords from its first vertex, and throw in \(33\) isolated vertices. That is \(66\) vertices, \(42\) edges and \(34\) components; its spanning forest has \(66-34=32\) edges, so \(42-32=10\) deletions are needed to make it acyclic, and \(34-1=33\) additions to connect it. Both were confirmed by brute‑force minimisation.
A word on the wording Taken hyper-literally, "at least 10 must be deleted" and "at least 33 must be added" would only say \(m-n+c\ge10\) and \(c-1\ge33\), which do not determine \(m\) — for instance \(66\) vertices in \(34\) components with a \(K_{33}\) among them satisfies both and has \(528\) edges. The question asks for the number of edges, so the two numbers are the exact minima, as used above.
Machine check Both formulas of Theorem 14.1 were checked against brute‑force minimisation on \(120\) random graphs with up to seven vertices, and the witness graph above was verified to have \(66\) vertices, \(42\) edges, \(34\) components, deletion number \(10\) and addition number \(33\).

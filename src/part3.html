PROBLEM 3

\(2\)-regular graphs, and graphs with \(\Delta(G)\le 2\)

classification
Problem

Determine the \(2\)-regular graphs. What are those graphs that satisfy \(\Delta(G)\le 2\)?

Both classes admit a complete and very clean description:

  • \(G\) is \(2\)-regular \(\iff\) \(G\) is a disjoint union of cycles;
  • \(\Delta(G)\le 2\) \(\iff\) \(G\) is a disjoint union of paths and cycles.

We prove both, in that order; the second builds on the first.

Part 1. The \(2\)-regular graphs

Theorem 3.1 A finite simple graph \(G\) is \(2\)-regular if and only if every component of \(G\) is a cycle; equivalently, if and only if \(G\) is a vertex‑disjoint union \(G\cong C_{n_1}\,\dot\cup\, C_{n_2}\,\dot\cup\,\dots\,\dot\cup\, C_{n_k}\) of cycles, with \(k\ge1\) and every \(n_i\ge 3\).
Proof of "\(\Leftarrow\)". In the cycle \(C_m\) every vertex has exactly the two neighbours adjacent to it along the cycle, so \(C_m\) is \(2\)-regular. Degrees are not affected by taking disjoint unions (a vertex acquires no new neighbours), so a disjoint union of cycles is \(2\)-regular. □
Proof of "\(\Rightarrow\)". Let \(G\) be \(2\)-regular and let \(H\) be any component of \(G\). By Lemma D, \(d_H(x)=d_G(x)=2\) for every \(x\in V(H)\), so \(H\) is itself connected and \(2\)-regular. In particular \(\delta(H)=2\), so Lemma C gives a cycle \(C\subseteq H\).

We claim \(H=C\). First, \(V(H)=V(C)\). Suppose not; since \(H\) is connected and \(V(C)\neq\emptyset\), there is an edge \(xy\in E(H)\) with \(x\in V(C)\) and \(y\notin V(C)\). But \(x\) lies on the cycle \(C\), so its two neighbours along \(C\) are already two distinct neighbours of \(x\), both inside \(V(C)\); as \(d_H(x)=2\) these are all of its neighbours, and \(y\notin V(C)\) cannot be one of them — a contradiction. Hence \(V(H)=V(C)\).

Second, \(E(H)=E(C)\). Each \(x\in V(C)\) already has its two \(C\)-neighbours, and \(d_H(x)=2\), so \(x\) is incident with no edge of \(H\) outside \(E(C)\). As this holds for every vertex, \(E(H)=E(C)\).

Therefore \(H=C\) is a cycle, and \(G\) is the disjoint union of its components, each a cycle. Finally, in a simple graph a cycle has length at least \(3\) (a length-\(2\) "cycle" would be a repeated edge, a length-1 one a loop), so every \(n_i\ge3\).

□
A second, purely arithmetic proof of "\(\Rightarrow\)" Let \(H\) be a component, \(|V(H)|=h\). By Lemma A applied to \(H\), \(2h=\sum_{v\in V(H)}d_H(v)=2|E(H)|\), so \(|E(H)|=h\): the component has exactly as many edges as vertices. A connected graph on \(h\) vertices has at least \(h-1\) edges, with equality exactly for trees; having \(h\) edges it contains exactly one cycle. Since \(\delta(H)=2\) forbids any vertex of degree \(1\), no tree‑like appendage can hang off that cycle, so \(H\) is the cycle itself.
Corollary (counting) A \(2\)-regular graph on \(n\) vertices has exactly \(n\) edges, and by Theorem 3.1 the \(2\)-regular graphs on \(n\) vertices correspond bijectively (up to isomorphism) to the partitions of \(n\) into parts of size \(\ge3\). For \(n=3,\dots,9\) the counts are \(1,1,1,2,2,3,4\); e.g. for \(n=8\) the three graphs are \(C_8\), \(C_5\,\dot\cup\,C_3\) and \(C_4\,\dot\cup\,C_4\). In particular there is no \(2\)-regular simple graph on \(1\) or \(2\) vertices.

Part 2. The graphs with \(\Delta(G)\le 2\)

Theorem 3.2 A finite simple graph \(G\) satisfies \(\Delta(G)\le 2\) if and only if every component of \(G\) is a path (possibly the trivial path \(K_1\)) or a cycle. Equivalently: \(G\) is a vertex‑disjoint union of paths and cycles.
Proof of "\(\Leftarrow\)". In \(P_k\) the two end vertices have degree \(1\) and all others degree \(2\) (in \(P_1=K_1\) the only vertex has degree \(0\)); in \(C_m\) every degree is \(2\). All these are \(\le2\), and disjoint unions do not change degrees. □
Proof of "\(\Rightarrow\)". Let \(H\) be a component of \(G\); by Lemma D every vertex of \(H\) has \(d_H=d_G\le 2\). We distinguish two cases.

Case 1: every vertex of \(H\) has degree exactly \(2\). Then \(H\) is a connected \(2\)-regular graph, so by Theorem 3.1 it is a cycle.

Case 2: some vertex \(z\in V(H)\) has \(d_H(z)\le 1\). We show \(H\) is a path. If \(|V(H)|=1\) then \(H=K_1\), the trivial path, and we are done; so assume \(|V(H)|\ge2\), whence \(d_H(z)=1\) (a vertex of degree \(0\) would be a component by itself). Build a walk greedily: \[ z=x_0,\quad x_1:=\text{the unique neighbour of } x_0, \quad x_{i+1}:=\text{the neighbour of } x_i \text{ other than } x_{i-1} \ \ (i\ge1), \] continuing as long as \(x_i\) has a second neighbour, i.e. as long as \(d_H(x_i)=2\).

The walk never revisits a vertex. Suppose it did, and let \(j\) be the least index with \(x_j\in\{x_0,\dots,x_{j-1}\}\), say \(x_j=x_i\) where \(i \lt j\). Note first that \(j\ge i+2\): the vertex \(x_{i+1}\) is a neighbour of \(x_i\) and \(H\) has no loops, so \(x_{i+1}\ne x_i\) and \(j\ne i+1\). Now the edge \(x_{j-1}x_j\) is incident with \(x_j=x_i\), so \(x_{j-1}\) is a neighbour of \(x_i\); we rule out every possibility for it.

  • \(i=0\). Here \(d_H(x_0)=1\) and the only neighbour of \(x_0\) is \(x_1\), so \(x_{j-1}=x_1\). If \(j=2\) this reads \(x_2=x_0\), contradicting the choice of \(x_2\) as the neighbour of \(x_1\) other than \(x_0\). If \(j\gt 2\) then \(x_{j-1}=x_1\) is itself a repetition, at the index \(j-1\lt j\), contradicting the minimality of \(j\).
  • \(i\ge 1\). The walk reached \(x_{i+1}\), so \(d_H(x_i)=2\) and \(x_i\) has exactly the two neighbours \(x_{i-1}\) and \(x_{i+1}\); hence \(x_{j-1}\) is one of them. If \(x_{j-1}=x_{i+1}\), then either \(j-1=i+1\), which reads \(x_{i+2}=x_i\) and contradicts the choice of \(x_{i+2}\) as the neighbour of \(x_{i+1}\) other than \(x_i\); or \(j-1\gt i+1\), and then \(x_{j-1}=x_{i+1}\) is a repetition at the index \(j-1\lt j\). If instead \(x_{j-1}=x_{i-1}\), then since \(j-1\ge i+1\gt i-1\) this is again a repetition at the index \(j-1\lt j\).

Every case contradicts either the construction of the walk or the minimality of \(j\), so no vertex repeats. Since \(H\) is finite the walk must therefore stop, and it stops at a vertex \(x_k\) with \(d_H(x_k)=1\). So \(P:=x_0x_1\dots x_k\) is a path in \(H\).

Finally \(H=P\). Every vertex of \(P\) has all of its \(H\)-neighbours on \(P\): the interior vertices \(x_1,\dots,x_{k-1}\) have degree \(2\) with both neighbours \(x_{i-1},x_{i+1}\) on \(P\), and the two ends \(x_0,x_k\) have degree \(1\) with their unique neighbour on \(P\). Therefore no edge leaves \(V(P)\); since \(H\) is connected this forces \(V(H)=V(P)\), and then \(E(H)=E(P)\) because every vertex of \(P\) has already used up its degree inside \(P\). So \(H=P\) is a path.

□
The counting version of Case 2 There is a slicker way to see that a tree \(T\) with \(\Delta(T)\le2\) is a path. Being acyclic and connected, \(T\) has \(|E(T)|=|V(T)|-1\); if \(|V(T)|\ge2\) then no degree is \(0\), so every degree is \(1\) or \(2\). Writing \(\ell\) for the number of degree‑\(1\) vertices, Lemma A gives \[ \ell + 2\bigl(|V(T)|-\ell\bigr) \;=\; \sum_{v}d(v) \;=\; 2|E(T)| \;=\; 2|V(T)|-2 ,\] i.e. \(2|V(T)|-\ell = 2|V(T)|-2\), so \(\ell=2\) exactly: a tree with maximum degree \(\le 2\) has precisely two leaves and is a path. (Case 2 of the proof above first checks that \(H\) is acyclic: a cycle in \(H\) would already use up both available degrees at each of its vertices, making \(H\) a cycle by the argument of Theorem 3.1 and contradicting the existence of \(z\) with \(d_H(z)\le1\).)
Answer

\(2\)-regular graphs are exactly the vertex‑disjoint unions of cycles \(C_{n_1}\dot\cup\dots\dot\cup C_{n_k}\), \(n_i\ge3\); equivalently, graphs all of whose components are cycles. Such a graph has as many edges as vertices.

Graphs with \(\Delta(G)\le 2\) are exactly the vertex‑disjoint unions of paths and cycles — every component is a path (an isolated vertex being the trivial path) or a cycle. Comparing the two statements: \(G\) is \(2\)-regular precisely when \(\Delta(G)\le2\) and \(\delta(G)\ge2\); relaxing \(\Delta\le2\) from regularity is exactly what admits the path components.

Remark (infinite graphs) If infinite graphs are allowed the classification acquires two extra items. A connected \(2\)-regular graph may also be a double ray \(\dots\,v_{-2}v_{-1}v_0v_1v_2\,\dots\), the two‑way infinite path; and a connected graph with \(\Delta\le2\) may in addition be a ray \(v_0v_1v_2\dots\) (one‑way infinite), whose initial vertex has degree \(1\). So in general: components of a \(2\)-regular graph are cycles or double rays, and components of a graph with \(\Delta\le2\) are finite paths, cycles, rays, or double rays. The ray reappears in Problem 4 as the counterexample to the odd‑degree theorem.

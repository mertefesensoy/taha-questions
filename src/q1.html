PROBLEM 9

Graphs with all degrees distinct

pigeonhole
Problem

Determine all finite simple graphs that do not have two vertices of the same degree.

Theorem 9.1 The only finite simple graph whose degrees are pairwise distinct is the one‑vertex graph \(K_1\).
Proof. For \(n=1\) the condition is vacuous, and \(K_1\) qualifies. So let \(G\) be simple with \(n\ge2\) vertices and suppose its \(n\) degrees are pairwise distinct.

In a simple graph a vertex has at most \(n-1\) neighbours, so every degree lies in the \(n\)-element set \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\). We have \(n\) pairwise distinct degrees drawn from a set of size \(n\), so every value in \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\) is taken, each exactly once. In particular there is a vertex \(u\) with \(d(u)=0\) and a vertex \(w\) with \(d(w)=n-1\), and \(u\ne w\) because \(0\ne n-1\) (here \(n\ge2\)).

Now \(d(w)=n-1\) means \(w\) is adjacent to all \(n-1\) other vertices, \(u\) among them. But then \(d(u)\ge 1\), contradicting \(d(u)=0\).

So no simple graph on \(n\ge2\) vertices has pairwise distinct degrees.

□
Answer

Only \(K_1\). For \(n\ge2\) the \(n\) distinct degrees would have to exhaust \(\{0,1,\dots,n-1\}\), producing simultaneously a vertex of degree \(0\) and a vertex of degree \(n-1\) — which is impossible, since the latter is adjacent to the former.

The usual formulation Turned around, the theorem says: every simple graph on at least two vertices has two vertices of equal degree. This is the standard "two people at a party have the same number of acquaintances" puzzle. Note where simplicity is used: with loops or parallel edges the degrees are no longer confined to \(\{0,\dots,n-1\}\) and the argument collapses — two vertices joined by one edge and by a second parallel edge attached to a loop can easily have different degrees.
Machine check Every simple graph on \(n\le 6\) vertices was enumerated: exactly one has pairwise distinct degrees, namely \(K_1\) at \(n=1\); for \(n=2,\dots,6\) there are none.
PROBLEM 10

The degree sequence 2, 2, 2, 4, 5, 5

forced adjacencies
Problem

Prove that if the degree sequence of a 6-vertex graph is \(2,2,2,4,5,5\), then it is not simple.

The two cheap tests are passed, so they cannot be the obstruction: the entries sum to \(2+2+2+4+5+5=20\), which is even (so \(m=10\)), and the largest entry \(5\) does not exceed \(n-1=5\). The obstruction is structural, and the two vertices of degree \(5\) are what force it.

Theorem 10.1 No simple graph on six vertices has degree sequence \(2,2,2,4,5,5\).
Proof. Suppose \(G\) were such a simple graph, on the vertex set \(\{a,b,c,d,x,y\}\) with \[ d(a)=d(b)=d(c)=2, \qquad d(d)=4, \qquad d(x)=d(y)=5 . \]

Step 1: \(x\) and \(y\) are joined to everything. Since \(G\) is simple on six vertices, a vertex has at most \(5\) neighbours, and they are distinct. So \(d(x)=5\) forces \(x\) to be adjacent to each of \(a,b,c,d,y\), and likewise \(y\) to each of \(a,b,c,d,x\).

Step 2: \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\) are used up. By Step 1 each of \(a,b,c\) is already adjacent to both \(x\) and \(y\), so each already has degree \(2\). As their prescribed degree is exactly \(2\), none of them is adjacent to anything else. In particular \[ ad,\; bd,\; cd \notin E(G). \]

Step 3: \(d\) cannot reach degree 4. By Step 1, \(d\) is adjacent to \(x\) and to \(y\); by Step 2 it is adjacent to none of \(a,b,c\). Since \(G\) is simple, \(d\) cannot be joined to \(x\) or \(y\) a second time, and there is no loop at \(d\). Hence \(d(d)=2\), contradicting \(d(d)=4\).

The assumption of simplicity is what fails, so no simple graph has this degree sequence.

□

The same conclusion from Havel–Hakimi

The reduction of Havel and Hakimi deletes the largest entry \(d_1\) and subtracts \(1\) from each of the next \(d_1\) entries; the original sequence is graphical if and only if the reduced one is. Applied here:

At the third step the leading entry \(2\) demands two further vertices of positive residual degree, and none remain. So the sequence is not graphical — in agreement with Theorem 10.1.

Answer

No simple graph has this degree sequence, so any graph realising \(2,2,2,4,5,5\) must contain a loop or a pair of parallel edges. The reason is that the two vertices of degree \(5=n-1\) are adjacent to everything; that already exhausts the degree of the three vertices of degree \(2\), leaving the vertex of prescribed degree \(4\) with only \(x\) and \(y\) available — degree \(2\).

The sequence is realisable if parallel edges are allowed Simplicity is exactly what fails, and nothing more. Taking the edges \[ xy,\; xd,\; xd,\; xa,\; xb,\; yd,\; yd,\; yc,\; ya,\; bc \] (with \(xd\) and \(yd\) each doubled) gives a loopless multigraph on \(a,b,c,d,x,y\) with ten edges and degrees \(d(x)=d(y)=5\), \(d(d)=4\), \(d(a)=d(b)=d(c)=2\), as required.
A third route: Erdős–Gallai The Erdős–Gallai criterion says a non-increasing sequence with even sum is graphical exactly when \(\sum_{i\le k} d_i \le k(k-1)+\sum_{i\gt k}\min(d_i,k)\) for every \(k\). For \(5,5,4,2,2,2\) the inequality holds at \(k=1,2,4,5,6\) but fails at \(k=3\): \[ 5+5+4 \;=\; 14 \qquad\text{versus}\qquad 3\cdot2+\min(2,3)+\min(2,3)+\min(2,3) \;=\; 6+6 \;=\; 12 . \] The three vertices of largest degree want \(14\) edge-endpoints, but they can absorb at most \(6\) among themselves and at most \(3\cdot2=6\) from the rest.
Machine check All \(2^{15}=32\,768\) simple graphs on six labelled vertices were enumerated; none has degree sequence \(2,2,2,4,5,5\). The multigraph above was checked to have the right degrees, and the Erdős–Gallai inequalities were evaluated for all six values of \(k\), failing exactly at \(k=3\).
PROBLEM 11

Eleven vertices and forty-five edges

averaging
Problem

Show that if \(G=(V,E)\) satisfies \(|V|=11\) and \(|E|=45\), then it has a vertex of degree at least 9.

Theorem 11.1 Every graph with \(11\) vertices and \(45\) edges has a vertex of degree at least \(9\).
Proof. By the handshake lemma (Lemma A), \[ \sum_{v\in V} d(v) \;=\; 2|E| \;=\; 2\cdot 45 \;=\; 90 . \] Suppose, for contradiction, that every vertex had degree at most \(8\). Summing this bound over the \(11\) vertices gives \[ 90 \;=\; \sum_{v\in V} d(v) \;\le\; 11\cdot 8 \;=\; 88 , \] which is false. Hence some vertex has degree at least \(9\). □

Equivalently: the average degree is \(90/11 = 8.\overline{18}\), and the maximum of a finite list is at least its average, so \(\Delta(G)\ge \lceil 90/11\rceil = 9\).

Answer

The degrees sum to \(2\cdot 45 = 90\). If all eleven were at most \(8\) the sum could not exceed \(11\cdot 8 = 88 < 90\). So some vertex has degree at least \(9\).

Two remarks The argument uses nothing about simplicity, so it holds for multigraphs as well. And the bound \(9\) cannot be improved: since \(\binom{11}{2}=55\), deleting the ten edges of a Hamiltonian path from \(K_{11}\) leaves a simple graph with \(11\) vertices and exactly \(45\) edges in which every degree is \(10\) minus the path degree, i.e. \(8\) or \(9\). So \(\Delta=9\) is attained and the conclusion "degree at least \(10\)" would be false.
